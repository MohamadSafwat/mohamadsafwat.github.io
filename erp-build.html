THE PROBLEM

Every customer question went through me

Sales needs a figure→Waits for the owner→Owner updates the sheet→Answer goes back→Customer is told→Next question…

An account balance, a statement, the price a customer paid last time — each one was a round trip of hours or days. Seven Excel workbooks held customers, suppliers, purchasing, expenses, capital and pricing. None of them talked to each other, and only I could reconcile them.

The constraint was not the data. It was that one person had to be available for anyone else to do their job.

ANALYSIS FIRST, THEN THE SYSTEM

Three models, each answering a question the business couldn't

1 · Pricing

Three numbers for every customer and product

Six years of transactions reduced to the lowest price that customer has accepted, the recommended price, and the highest charged and accepted.

2 · Finance

Profit and cash, seen for the first time

Every invoice, payment, expense and capital movement in one workbook — profit, receivables and return on working capital, monthly.

3 · Dashboard

The operating numbers, every week

Sales, margin, collections and stock tracked continuously — not assembled once a year for the accountant.

Only then was it clear what the system had to do. The ERP was not the starting point — it is where the three models were put together so the whole team could use them, not just me.

THE PRICING MODEL

Back-tested against two years of real transactions

Had the recommended price been used instead of my pricing from memory, the average sale would have been 21.5% higher in 2025 and 13.1% higher in the first five months of 2026 — without ever going below the last price a customer accepted.

Average uplift of the recommended price vs prices actually charged.

Method

For each customer–product pair, the floor is the lowest margin that relationship has accepted. The recommendation adds the median gap above it; the ceiling adds the 90th percentile.

The rule that matters

The recommendation never undercuts the last price charged. In a relationship market, a lower price says the old one was unfair — and trains customers to wait for discounts.

Fallback

With no history for a customer, the model uses the cross-customer rate for that product, then a tier default. Every price carries a confidence level.
